Discussion 
118 
CRPS remains a frequent postoperative complication1. It is distributed differently according to the different series10-
119 
13. Its frequency can reach 37 percent14. Contrary to commonly accepted notions, psychological and constitutional 
120 
factors cannot explain its occurrence2,14-16. In fact, the experimental study of Van der Laan et al.2 shows the toxicity 
121 
of free radicals released during soft-tissue contusion that increase the vascular permeability of striated muscle to 
122 
macromolecules thereby decreasing circulating proteins and fluids17. Matsuda et al.3 observed the beneficial effect 
123 
of vitamin C in high doses, which reduces leakage of liquids and proteins thanks to its antioxidant and protective 
124 
action against capillary endothelium, red blood cells and leucocytes18. Regarding prevention efficacy, a systematic 
125 
review of the literature associated with a meta-analysis performed by Aïm et al.6 found only three randomized 
126 
controlled trials. Two series are in favour of the preventive character of vitamin C intake to offset the occurrence of 
127 
the CRPS4,19 whereas one study shows no benefit from administration of vitamin C as a preventive measure20. 
128 
Chronologically, in 1999, Zollinger et al.4 showed in a study on two series of patients that vitamin C administration 
129 
in the event of reduced and immobilized distal radius fracture prevented the development of CRPS. In 2002, we 
130 
reported in a retrospective sequenced study that daily intake of one gram of vitamin C for 45 days in distal radius 
131 
trauma enabled reducing the rate of CRPS from 10 to 2.1%5. In 2004, Reuben et al.21 concluded that only vitamin C 
132 
has proven its effectiveness in preventing the development of CRPS, in scheduled orthopaedic surgery. To conclude, 
133 
in 2009, Besse et al.22 confirmed the preventive effect of vitamin C in CRPS, on ankle and foot surgery. These again 
134 
are retrospective studies. Regarding the dosage to be prescribed, in 2007, Zollinger et al.19 showed that intake of 
135 
doses below 500 mg are ineffective. In 2013, Shibuya et al.23 demonstrated through a meta-analysis the beneficial 
136 
effects and non-toxicity of high-dose vitamin C in the prevention of CRPS for lower limb trauma. In 2017, Aïm et 
137 
al.6 using a systematic review and a meta-analysis of only placebo-controlled randomized trials, concluded that 
138 
taking 500 mg of vitamin C daily for 50 days tended to reduce pain syndrome by half. This refers to oral intake of 
139 
7 
 
vitamin C, as only 10% of the ingested dose is absorbed. With reference to the usefulness of liposomal encapsulation: 
140 
in 2017, Sabesan et al.24 indicated that liposomal encapsulation used in shoulder surgery anaesthesia to deliver 
141 
constant doses of bupivacaine for 72 hours provided excellent control of postoperative pain by drastically reducing 
142 
opioid consumption. Our study has shown that vitamin C in its liposomal form seems even more effective than the 
143 
vitamin C usually and the placebo administered to prevent the occurrence of the CRPS and no case was found 
144 
throughout the one-year follow-up with p < 0.05 (Table 3). The liposomal envelope provides protection against 
145 
acidic gastric juices and intestinal enzymes thus allowing almost complete absorption, without denaturing the active 
146 
ingredient.
